About me

My name is Qays Judge. I'm a third-year economist at the University of Cambridge. I've worked as a quantitative trader in commodities at DV Trading, and in summer 2027 I'm joining Balyasny as a quantitative analyst intern, also in commodities.

I have a strong interest in meteorology: how weather systems form and move, and what extreme weather does to the people and economies in its path. As my work has become more technical, my interest has moved from how weather works to how to model it.

Extreme weather is becoming more frequent and more severe, and its heaviest costs fall on the communities least able to absorb them. I want to work somewhere deeply statistical, forecasting those extremes, so that the right people know when, where and how hard they are likely to hit.

To work towards that, I've taken on three projects, each looking further ahead than the last.

“Chaos: when the present determines the future, but the approximate present does not approximately determine the future.”

Edward Lorenz, meteorologist and founder of chaos theory

In 1961 Lorenz re-ran a weather simulation from a printout that had rounded one number from 0.506127 to 0.506. Within a couple of simulated months, the two forecasts had nothing in common. The two lines drawn here are that experiment: the same equations, started a hair's breadth apart. It is the reason modern forecasts are ensembles of many possible futures, and the reason every one of my projects forecasts a probability rather than a single answer.

Project 124–72 hours ahead · Pakistan · Sep 2026

Probabilistic Extreme-Monsoon Rainfall Forecasting

Aim

If heavy monsoon rain is coming in the next three days, can a model tell us how likely the extremes are, not just how much rain to expect?

The 2022 monsoon left huge areas of Pakistan underwater. For anyone deciding whether to evacuate, open a barrage or move livestock, a single "best guess" rainfall map isn't enough. What matters is the chance of the extreme.

Methodology

  1. Build the data

    24 years of ERA5 atmospheric fields and NASA IMERG satellite rainfall, aligned onto one 0.25° grid with xarray and Dask. Training windows are leakage-controlled, so no information from the future reaches the model.

  2. Predict the mean, then generate the rest

    A ConvLSTM–U-Net predicts the expected rainfall field. A conditional diffusion model then learns what that mean misses, such as displaced rain bands and storm intensity, and samples 30 realistic rainfall scenarios.

  3. Test honestly

    Held-out 2022–25 monsoons, including the 2022 floods, scored with CRPS and Brier skill against quantile XGBoost, a strong baseline that predicts rainfall quantiles directly. U-Net versions without diffusion isolate what diffusion adds.

Results

Overall forecast error (CRPS)
−11%

Lower error than quantile XGBoost across every rainfall amount, from dry days to deluges.

Skill on extreme-rain days (Brier skill)
0.14 → 0.21

50% more skill at calling the heaviest 5% of rain days, which is an 8% lower Brier score.

Project 21–7 days ahead · Indus Basin · Sep 2026

River-Network Graph Neural Networks for Flood Forecasting

Aim

Rain becomes a flood only once it reaches the river. If a model knows what flows into what, and how long it takes, does it forecast floods better, and can its probabilities drive better warnings?

Water falling on the Kashmir hills reaches Punjab days later. Standard sequence models treat each point on the river on its own; I encoded the Indus as a directed graph so information passes downstream the way water does.

Methodology

  1. Turn the river into a graph

    64 nodes on the Indus and its tributaries, built from GloFAS drainage-direction data. Each edge means "drains into" and carries a travel-time lag. Nodes hold rainfall, temperature, soil wetness and simulated discharge.

  2. Pass messages downstream

    Each node keeps a GRU memory and receives lagged messages from upstream in PyTorch Geometric. It outputs discharge quantiles and flood-exceedance probabilities 1–7 days ahead, with a hydrologically motivated penalty on implausible flows.

  3. Test the structure, then the decisions

    Compared on held-out years with an LSTM that already receives upstream data, so any gain comes from the graph itself. Shuffled and reversed edges test whether the real topology matters. Probabilities are turned into cost-aware warnings.

Results

Discharge accuracy (NSE)
0.78 → 0.84

27% less squared error in daily river flow than the upstream-informed LSTM.

Flood-probability error (Brier)
0.031 → 0.025

19% lower error on the probability of crossing flood thresholds.

Modelled warning loss
−20%

Cost-aware alerts beat a fixed "more likely than not" rule.

Shuffling or reversing the river's edges reduced skill, so the real direction of flow is doing the work.

Project 3ECMWF AI Weather Quest · Week 3 · Global · Nov 2026

Compute-Efficient Ensemble Transformers for Sub-seasonal Forecasting

Aim

Three weeks out, most weather predictability is gone. Can a model sharpen ECMWF's own ensemble into better rainfall probabilities, and do it cheaply enough to run routinely?

The ECMWF AI Weather Quest is a competition in forecasting weekly rainfall three weeks ahead across the globe. I explored post-processing ECMWF's ensemble with a transformer, then shrinking that model without losing its skill.

Methodology

  1. Learn from 17 years of reforecasts

    ECMWF's 11-member reforecasts from 2004–2020 as inputs and ERA5 weekly rainfall as truth, on the competition's 1.5° global grid over land. The task is to predict which fifth of the historical range each week's rainfall falls in.

  2. Attend across the ensemble

    A permutation-equivariant transformer treats ensemble members as an unordered set and attends across them. Trained directly on the ranked probability score, it runs on the 101-member operational ensemble without retraining.

  3. Shrink it

    A 12M-parameter teacher is distilled into a 4M-parameter student, using BF16 precision and efficient attention. Speed and memory are measured on the same GPU, and skill is checked with block-bootstrap confidence intervals.

Results

Week-3 rainfall skill (RPSS)
0.019 → 0.031

1.6× the skill of ECMWF's raw IFS ensemble on held-out 2023–24, at a range where a few hundredths is a real gain.

Skill kept after distillation
98%

A student a third the size (12M → 4M parameters) keeps almost all of the teacher's skill.

Compute, student vs teacher
2.3×

Faster inference, with 45% less peak GPU memory.
